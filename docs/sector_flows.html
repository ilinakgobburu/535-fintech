Tracking tech's ripple effect on the market

NVIDIA lost 23% of its value in the five sessions of November 16–20, 2026. This page looks at what the other ten sectors did that week and how much of it tech's move explains: through prices (each sector's beta to tech), money moving into and out of funds (ETF creations and redemptions), and options (implied volatility). On Monday, November 23, NVIDIA rebounded 31%, back above where the week began; section 7 looks at what the rest of the market did that day. Section 4 measures the ten largest tech stocks themselves: value lost, buyer- and seller-initiated trading, short-marked volume and short interest, options and implied vol, and each fund's beta to the ten.

Tiles are price changes from the November 13 close to the November 20 close; the Monday figures run from the November 20 close to the November 23 close. The charts below use log returns, which add up across days. Data from LSEG.

1 · Beta

How much each sector usually moves with tech

A sector's beta to tech is the slope of its daily return on XLK's, measured over the two years before last week. It plays the role an option's delta plays: how much the sector moves per 1% move in tech. A beta of 0.30 means the sector moved 0.30% on average for every 1% XLK moved; R² shows how loosely. Gamma and vega carry the option analogy further: gamma is how that beta changes on big tech moves, and vega is how the sector's return has gone with changes in tech's implied volatility, with XLK's own move held fixed.

Sector greeks to tech

Estimate (dot), ±1 standard error (bar), ±2 (line) Gamma without April 2025*

Each candle is one estimate: the dot is the value, the bar covers one standard error either side and the thin line two (about a 95% range). A candle that crosses the zero line means the data can't tell the sign. Gamma is slightly positive for nine of ten sectors over the two years, mostly because of the April 2025 tariff crash and rebound (April 3–10). *Without those six sessions (a check added after seeing the results), every sector's gamma is within ±0.015 except energy's (−0.064 ± 0.023), and the standard errors grow by about 70%, so outside that episode the data can't pin down any curvature. Vega is negative everywhere: a 1-point rise in XLK's implied vol has gone with a 0.17–0.29% lower return for the other sectors, beyond what tech's own move explains. Flow beta is a fund's daily net flow as posted, in % of its shares, per 1% XLK move the session before. Theta has no counterpart here (a fund doesn't decay against tech), so it is left out.

2 · Last week

Nine of ten sectors fell more than their beta said, but not on tech's worst day

For each fund, the gray ring is what its beta predicted from XLK's five daily moves, and the blue dot is what actually happened. Nine of the ten sectors fell more than predicted: by 2 to 5.5 points for eight of them and 1.7 for industrials. Consumer discretionary, the most tech-like sector, fell 0.3 points less.

Week return = the sum of the five daily log returns, in %. The orange diamond adds vega (a prediction added after seeing the results; tech's implied vol rose 16 points over the week). It brings the week's prediction 20–48% closer for the nine sectors that fell more than predicted, but it doesn't track the days: it overshoots Friday's drop for every sector by 0.4 to 1.8 points, and for consumer discretionary it moves the week's prediction too low. The registered beta-plus-gamma prediction does no better; it widens the gap for every sector but energy. Treasuries (TLT) rose 2.6% when beta predicted a small fall, and gold (GLD) fell 9.2% in price.

The extra losses came before Friday

On Friday, when XLK fell 7.3%, every sector landed within 0.3 points of its beta prediction or fell less than predicted (financials −1.8% against −2.1% predicted). The gap built up mostly Tuesday to Thursday, when XLK moved −0.9%, +0.3% and −1.9% (energy and communication services also missed on Monday). On Wednesday, with tech up, energy fell 3.3% and health care 2.2%. These data don't say what drove those days. This day-by-day split was added after seeing the results.

How unusual was the miss?

A single day's miss has a standard deviation of about 1 point (0.9 to 1.5 by sector, and 1.2 to 2.5 on days tech fell 2% or more), so the fair yardstick for a five-day miss is every five-day stretch of the past two years (500 of them). Last week put seven sectors in their worst 10% (health care at the 1.2nd percentile, communication services 3.4th, financials 3.6th), industrials and utilities near their 18th percentile, and consumer discretionary above its median. Averaged over the ten sectors, the miss was −3.1 points, worse than all but 1.6% of five-day stretches. The worst was the week ending April 8, 2025 (−8.7). This comparison was added after seeing the results.

Beta is also lower lately

The two-year beta includes April 2025, when everything moved together. Over the 63 sessions before last week, most sectors had little or no beta to tech (financials 0.07, utilities −0.02), and staples' was negative (−0.25). Measured that way, the gaps above would be even bigger. See the tracker in section 5.

3 · Money flows

Monday to Thursday: out of most sector funds and gold, more than usual into long Treasuries

Each ETF's net flow is the change in its shares outstanding times its NAV: shares are created when money comes in and redeemed when it leaves. The first chart below lines up the funds that lost money against the funds that gained; the second shows each fund's flow against its own history. Through Thursday, money did not rotate into defensive sectors as a group. By trade date, health care lost $912m, more than in 98% of four-day stretches for that fund, and staples $154m, while utilities took in $37m and real estate $30m; as posted, staples took in $230m.

Energy lost $1.38bn, more than in 99% of four-day stretches for that fund, gold $1.37bn (more than in 95%) and financials $588m. Long Treasuries (TLT) took in $959m, more than usual (89th percentile), and T-bills (SGOV) $2.06bn, about normal for a fund that grows most weeks. Communication services (XLC) took in $319m, $257m of it on Wednesday. Tech itself (XLK) swung between inflows and outflows and ended the four days about flat (−$75m), after a $410m outflow on Thursday.

Flows can't be traced from one fund to another, and creations during a selloff can also meet short sellers' demand for shares, so these data show where money moved, not who moved it or why. For 13 of the 15 funds, Friday's trades, the day of the 7% tech drop, had not posted when the share counts were pulled again on Nov 24.

6 · Options

Implied vol rose in every sector

Each fund's 30-day at-the-money implied volatility (LSEG's ATMIV series) rose over the week, and S&P 500 implied vol nearly doubled. The gray ring is the rise each fund's normal vol beta predicted from XLK's (the slope of its daily IV change on XLK's). Real estate's rose 18 points against about 3 predicted, and financials' 12.8 against 8.9, about as far above as the S&P 500's (14.5 against 11.1). Energy (6.9 against 10.9) and consumer discretionary (11.0 against 13.0) rose less than predicted. Materials', utilities' and real estate's series are noisy, so read those three with care: materials' swung between 18 and 108 over the past three months and was below its starting level on Thursday, and utilities' printed 88 one day in September.

What that does to an option's greeks

Black-Scholes greeks of a fresh 30-day at-the-money call on $100 of each fund, priced at each end of the week (rate 0, no dividends). On SPY, such a call cost twice as much on Friday as one bought the Friday before ($1.71 → $3.37), and its daily theta doubled. Gamma per 1% move halved, because the higher vol spreads the same convexity over a wider range. ATM vega hardly depends on vol, so it barely changed.

7 · Monday

Monday: NVIDIA made back its whole loss; most other sectors moved with their betas and made back part

On Monday, November 23, NVIDIA's price rose 31.5% ($125.53 → $165.05) and closed 1.4% above its November 13 close. Its beta to XLK accounts for about 11 of its 27 log points; the rest was specific to NVIDIA, a miss of 9.5 standard deviations. XLK's price rose 9.0%, which still left it 4.6% below its November 13 close: it made back about two-thirds of the week's loss.

Most other sectors moved with their betas. Nine of the ten landed within 0.8 standard deviations of their beta prediction; in log returns, industrials rose 3.83% against 3.86% predicted, and the S&P 500 fund 4.60% against 4.58%. Energy was the exception, falling 1.06% where beta predicted a 1.47% rise (−1.7 standard deviations). Because Monday's moves matched beta, last week's extra losses, which had built up beyond what beta predicted, were still there at Monday's close.

8 · Method

How this was measured

Data and checks

Data
LSEG (platform session), requests in all (193 of them for section 4, whose sources are under "The ten"). Daily closes and volume for 15 ETFs and NVDA, and NAV for 13 of the ETFs (XLRE's and SGOV's flows use the close); daily shares outstanding (TR.SharesOutstanding); 30-day ATM implied vol (ATMIV.U) for 11 sector funds, SPY and NVDA. Baseline: the 504 sessions ending Nov 13 (section 4: the 252 sessions ending Nov 13, from Dec 1, 2025 for its 1-minute measures; its betas use the 504).
Written down first
The funds, the two-year baseline and the beta and flow measures were written down before any data was pulled. The greeks, the 63-session tracker and the implied-vol pull were added after the first price and share pull, before any of them was computed. Four things were added after seeing the results and are labeled where they appear: the beta + vega prediction, gamma without April 2025, and in section 2 the five-day comparison and the day-by-day split.
Flow timing
Share counts usually post a day late. For seven of the ten sector funds, flows line up with the fund's own return the day before (correlation 0.16–0.35), which fits a one-day reporting lag; it doesn't show up for XLK, XLC, XLU, XLRE, SPY, SGOV or TLT. The trade-date view shifts every fund's counts back one session. Both views are shown, and neither was chosen in advance.
Friday's flows
LSEG posts share counts about a day late. A second pull on Nov 24 (one more request, written down before it was made) filled in Thursday's trades; a count posted after Nov 20 is valued at the Nov 20 NAV. Friday's trades, the day of the crash, had still not posted for 13 of the 15 funds (their Nov 23 count repeated Nov 20's), so they are left out, not counted as zero.
Monday
Monday's prices and implied vol were pulled on Nov 24 (29 more requests), with the measures for section 7 written down before the pull. Every Monday prediction uses the two-year coefficients, not refitted. Where that pull also returned Nov 20 values (12 of the 29 series), they matched the earlier pulls. Monday's flows are not shown: share counts post a day or more late.
The ten
Section 4 was asked for on Nov 24 and written down before its data was pulled on Nov 27, then revised once, still before the pull, after three independent reviews, and amended twice after the LSEG pull, before any measure was computed or any 1-minute, FINRA, OCC or implied-vol value was looked at, to replace a refused report-date field. The names come from a rule written into a small probe before it pulled any market value (the ten largest of 36 US tech stocks by LSEG market value at the Nov 13 close); the week's price moves and the ETF flows were already known. Sources: LSEG (market value, shares, daily and 1-minute prices and volume, exchange short interest, 30-day ATM implied vol, report dates); FINRA's public daily short-sale volume files; OCC's public daily options volume by account type. Session times are converted to New York time; the two early-close days of the past year and any session whose 1-minute volume missed the day's total by more than 2% are left out of the baselines. The platform session refused LSEG's event-date field, so report dates come from TR.EPSActReportDate. While the free sources were being tested, three sets of NVIDIA figures were seen: four small customer put volumes on an adjusted series (Nov 9, 16, 17 and 18), which are a small part of the customer put counts in section 4; a short-interest ratio dated Nov 26 that turned out to be the Oct 30 value; and six open-interest lines for Nov 25 puts, which are not used. Nothing in the plan was changed after seeing them. One week is one event: the ranks in section 4 say how unusual each day was, not why. With ten names, four measures and eight days, a few extreme single-name figures are expected by chance, so the ten's totals are read first and single-name extremes are described, not tested. The 1-minute classification rules misclassify some volume; the checks show how much the answer depends on the rule.
Splits
XLK, XLE, XLU, XLY and XLB split 2-for-1 on Dec 5, 2025. LSEG's prices are split-adjusted but its share counts are not, so earlier counts were doubled. Otherwise each fund would show a fake inflow worth its whole size on that day.
Limits
One week is one event. ETF flows are only part of the money moving (mutual funds and futures aren't seen, and single stocks have no creations or redemptions: section 4 shows their trading, not money in or out), and some creations are arbitrage, not investors. NVDA reported earnings after the close on Nov 17; its first reaction (Nov 18) was −2.9%, and the 14% drop came on Friday. These data don't say why.
