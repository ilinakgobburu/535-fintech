Covered Call · —

This page backtests a covered call on a single name: long 100 shares, short one weekly call, held to expiry without rolls. Because the strategy itself is fixed, the analysis concentrates on three implementation decisions: the strike, the fill price and the time of the order. Each is recorded explicitly in the book, and its effect on the result is measured.

Framework

Each rule below is executed by a single loop in lib/covered_call.py. All parameters were fixed before any results were observed; none was chosen by hand or selected after the fact.

  1. capital The assignment does not specify starting capital, so the account is sized to the strategy. It is funded with — before the first order: —. This equals the cost of the first covered call, so cash is $0 once the first position is established and no capital is idle. An earlier version of this page used a flat $50,000, most of which was idle cash that diluted the return and obscured the margin mechanics; trades and trading P&L are unaffected by the choice.
  2. entry On the first session of each week (usually, but not always, Monday; see Methods), at the —: if the book holds no shares, buy 100 shares at the stock print. Cash decreases.
  3. strike In the same bar, sell one call expiring at the end of the same week, at the lowest listed strike at or above spot. The rule is "at or above" rather than "strictly above", so that when spot equals a listed strike the at-the-money strike is selected rather than the next strike out.
  4. fill The call is filled at mid = (BID + ASK) / 2 in the same bar. Both legs are priced from one bar, so neither uses information that postdates the other. If the chosen strike has no two-sided quote, no order is filled; because the stock and the call form a single decision, the stock is not purchased either.
  5. wait Positions are held to expiry, with no rolls, buy-to-close orders or other adjustments. At the official close of the week's last session:
    ST > K → assigned. The shares are delivered, cash increases by 100 × K, the book is flat, and the next week opens a new position.
    ST ≤ K → the call expires worthless. The shares and the premium are retained, and the next week's call is written against the shares already held.
Ties. If the stock closes exactly at the strike, the call has no intrinsic value and is treated as expiring unexercised.

Blotter

The blotter records only executed trades; it contains no working orders or signals. Each row cites the rule that produced it. Prices are shown at the precision at which they were booked: the stock's last print is frequently sub-penny, and a mid between two nickel quotes can end in a half cent, so quantity × fill reproduces each row's cash change exactly.

Ledger

The ledger reports shares, the short call (strike and expiry), cash and marks at the close of every session. It is derived from the blotter rather than maintained separately, so the two cannot diverge: cash at any bar equals starting cash plus the cash changes of all blotter events up to that bar, which a test verifies at each of the — bars.

Reg T margin

Margin is computed under Reg T rather than portfolio margin. Reg T can be computed without a broker's risk engine, and a book that satisfies Reg T also satisfies portfolio margin, whereas the converse does not hold. Reg T is therefore the more conservative requirement to report.

LMV = shares × stock mark NAV = cash + stock MV + option MV (a short call is NEGATIVE) − accrued margin interest (owed to the broker, not yet debited) initial = 50% × LMV covered short call adds $0 maint = 25% × LMV FINRA available = NAV − initial excess = NAV − maint

The covered call adds nothing to the initial requirement, because the short call's only obligation is to deliver shares the account already holds. Cash changes only on blotter events: stock purchases, premium received, expiry at zero and assignment at the strike. Marks change NAV but not cash. Accrued margin interest is the only other item that reduces NAV without a blotter row: it is a liability on the borrowed cash, deducted from NAV while cash remains as the blotter left it.

Mid versus trade price: scatter and R²

Every call in the book is filled at the mid, so this assumption is tested directly. The sample comprises every hourly bar in which a call had both a two-sided quote and a trade: — bars.

Fit within narrow price bands

A pooled R² across contracts priced from one cent to ninety-nine dollars is inflated by the price range alone. The fit is therefore re-estimated within narrow price bands, where distinguishing a $3 option from a $6 option is a more demanding test. The fit holds within these bands, contrary to the initial expectation.

Alternative strike rules

The booked rule is the assignment's baseline. The alternatives run through the identical engine (the same calendar, fills and ledger), so differences between them reflect the decision rule rather than differences in implementation. Two of the rules do not use a fixed distance: they select the strike that the week's at-the-money implied volatility prices at a stated probability of being breached, using the Black-76 inversion developed for Assignment 1.1.

Validation against one-minute data

Two claims on this page depend on how an hourly bar is constructed: that its BID/ASK is the quote at the end of the hour, and that prints outside their bar's quote are a timing artefact. To test both, the same contracts were re-pulled at one-minute resolution (— two-sided quoted bars).
